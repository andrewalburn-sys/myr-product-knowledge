MYR · AI Strategy
April 2026

Not a recipe app
with AI features.

A cooking companion — from the dinner question to the dinner table.

The Core Strategic Answer

One shared brain

A single intelligence layer that learns about your taste, household, constraints, and history, across every touchpoint.

Multiple role-specific surfaces

Different expressions of that intelligence depending on where you are in the journey, not a generic AI assistant slapped on top.

Three tiers of visibility

Invisible by default. Ambient at key moments. Explicit companion only for users who opt in. Never decorative.

The Three Things We Must Align On

1

Trust before the shortcut

We want to build an experience that reduces the mental load of figuring out dinner, quickly and confidently. But users will not let the product narrow that decision until they already trust what we surface. Building that trust layer comes first, and it reorders everything else.

2

Reliability is a product strategy

Several of our best opportunities are only transformative if the answer is trustworthy. Shipping before that bar is met doesn't get us to value faster: it destroys trust we can't quickly rebuild.

3

12–18 month competitive window

LLMs can already answer "what should I make tonight?" We have a window based on real recipe content, social proof, and personalization. It is closing.

The trust tolerance finding: Users are forgiving during browsing and discovery. They require near-perfect reliability during cooking and recovery. We cannot hold all stages to the same performance standard, and the stages with the highest stakes are the ones we have the least surface access to today.

Why the ranking looks this way

Why Discover + Decide leads

Most product investment today targets browsing and results quality, the top of Discover. But the pain concentrates at the commit end: evaluating fit, building conviction, choosing. Better browsing without better conviction signals doesn't close the gap. The whole zone needs to pull toward commitment.

Why Cook/Execute is the biggest whitespace

When a cook is mid-recipe and stuck: wrong ingredient, ambiguous step, timing collision — there is no product that solves this well. Users context-switch to Google, call someone, or improvise. No app is present in this moment in a useful way. That is the market gap. The voice companion (Phase 2 opt-in) is our first move into it.

Why passive inspiration ranks separately

Different user state, different success metric. The user has no decision intent: they're building appetite, not committing to anything. The goal is generating high-quality saves and future signal. Treating it as the same problem as active-intent discovery leads to the wrong product investment.

Discover Has Three Distinct Modes

Active-intent discovery

Users often can't articulate what they want, and even when they try, vague intent is misunderstood, results are overwhelming, and nothing creates enough conviction to commit. The product has to understand intent before it's fully formed, not wait for a precise query.

Passive inspiration (homepage)

The user is not solving dinner yet. Appetite needs building. Today the problem is weak relevance, generic packaging, and low save quality. Strategic job: generate high-quality saves and future decision signal.

Decision support from known signal

When the user is trying to decide what to make, the strongest starting point is often what they have already saved, cooked, or shown preference for. The system should use that existing signal first before reaching more broadly across the catalog.

The brain

Primary audience: Established Home Cook (50% of users). Everyone benefits, but this is their whole experience.

Tier 2 · Ambient

Smart product moments

AI surfaces as native product features, not labeled as AI. Constraint-first search, consolidated shopping lists, substitution suggestions. It just works.

A companion the user activates while looking at a recipe: substitution help, technique clarification, real-time guidance. Never the default. Launches on brand pages via OpenAI Realtime API before native recipes exist; deepens as the platform evolves.

What the Shared Brain Must Know

Now — connect what exists

  • Recipe metadata (taste, technique, occasion, effort, similarity)
  • Behavioral signals: saves, revisits, ratings, time-on-page
  • Click-out and return behavior as execution proxies

Later — as native recipes unlock

  • Pantry and ingredient state
  • Weekly plan and schedule state
  • Step-level cooking progress and substitution requests
  • Trust history: what worked, what was modified, what failed
The design rule: AI should only surface when it creates a meaningful shift in confidence, speed, or resilience. If removing the AI label does not change the user experience, the label should be removed.

Finding 3 · The Reliability-Gated 10x Rule

Reliability-gated 10x

These are 10x only if the AI answer is trustworthy. An untrustworthy version of the same feature is actively harmful: it fails at a high-stakes moment and damages trust we cannot quickly rebuild.

  • Dinner Decision Engine recommendations
  • In-the-moment substitution rescue
  • Pantry-aware intelligence (also data-gated)
Shipping rule: Do not ship until internal accuracy thresholds are confirmed. "We'll improve after launch" is not viable here.

Forgiving-threshold 10x

Step-function improvements where partial accuracy still delivers the value, where imperfection doesn't cause active harm and can be corrected. Still requires meaningful quality; just a looser bar than reliability-gated.

  • Constraint-adaptive full week plan generation: an imperfect meal in a plan is adjustable; no plan at all is not
  • Plan recovery engine (narrower audience: System Thinker + Weeknight Reducer)
Shipping rule: Ship when the output is coherent and constraint-aware. Value survives imperfection, but zero constraint awareness is not 10x.

Four phases.
Clear gates.

The product needs to observe what happens after each session — what got cooked, what got skipped, what was returned to — so the shared brain gets smarter without asking users to rate or review anything. This is the infrastructure that makes personalization compound over time.

Phase 1 is not invisible to users. These features ship incrementally as they're ready, they do not wait for the full phase to complete.

  • Smarter homepage: personalized, contextual, stronger module packaging that improves with every session

Competitive Landscape · Research-Based

Two categories.
One is mature. One is open.

The cooking app market has split into Recipe Destinations and Cooking Companions. Destinations are mature and crowded. The Companion category has been attempted by multiple players, and not won by any of them.

Recipe Destinations

Compete on editorial depth, passive discovery, and content trust. Stop at the moment you close the app to go cook.

NYT Cooking Pinterest TikTok / Instagram

Posture: complementary. These own browsing and passive inspiration. MyRecipes captures the save and owns everything after it.

Cooking Companions — Early Stage

Same category we're entering. Voice and execution attempted. Intelligence not yet cracked: even purpose-built voice apps consistently fail on real substitution reasoning.

Sprig Sous Foodie Tamarin SideChef

Posture: the space to win. None connect to a personal recipe library. Having a voice button is table stakes. The moat is what it can actually reason about.

Large Language Models

Fast improvement. No persistent memory. No personal library. Public research supports the broad threat, but not a clean apples-to-apples ranking of cooking hallucination rates across models.

What public research supports

Recipe generation still diverges from authoritative recipes

In one 2025 quantitative evaluation, GPT-4 ingredient lists matched authoritative recipes at 67-88%, directions at 64-86%, and diabetes-specific ingredient match varied from 26-83%.

Food-safety alignment is still weak

A 2026 food-safety benchmark found that current LLMs are not reliably aligned to food-safety risks. When safeguards fail, they can produce instructions that sound usable but are actually unsafe.

Sources: [1] Panangadan et al., Quantitative Evaluation of AI-Generated Recipes for Health Recommender Systems, IEEE / NSF PAR, 2025. [2] Luo et al., Cooking Up Risks: Benchmarking and Reducing Food Safety Risks in Large Language Models, arXiv 2026.

Posture: 12–18 month window. The trust gap in cooking is still real. LLMs will keep improving, but today public research still supports conviction, provenance, and personalization as defensible advantages. Build those now.

Planning & Utility Apps

Solve specific stages well. None plan from a user's personal recipe library: the universal gap.

Mealime 7M users Kitchendary 60s plan AnyList Instacart Smart Shop

Posture: partners and benchmarks. Instacart is the shopping partner. Every competitor plans from a catalog of ~1,000 recipes. Ours has 250,000 — informed by your complete save history as a taste signal.

Three Findings That Sharpen Strategy

No one owns execution

Cook/Execute and Recover have zero "Strong" competitor ratings across 16 players researched. Even purpose-built voice apps consistently return generic swaps or ignore downstream cooking consequences. The whitespace is confirmed, not assumed.

NYT Cooking is the ceiling

12.3M subscribers. 25,000+ editor-tested recipes. AI scaling launched January 2026. Zero execution layer — no planning, no voice, no substitution help. A developer built an unofficial "NYT Sous Chef" on Devpost. Destination is a ceiling, not a direction.

250,000 recipes vs. ~1,000 — a categorically different plan

Every competitor's plan generator is catalog-constrained — so is ours. But Mealime plans from ~1,000 recipes. Kitchendary from its own catalog. Ours draws from 250,000, informed by your full save history — including external saves — as a taste signal. The plan reflects what you actually cook, not a generic rotation.

The LLM Threat — Detail View

LLMs are already answering "what should I make tonight?" directly, naturally, and fast. Once that habit forms, it is the hardest firing behavior to reverse.

LLM weaknesses today

Real, tested recipe content Limited / fabrication risk
Social proof and trust signals None
Persistent personalization Session-only today
Dedicated cooking UI Recipe view only — rest of journey unoptimized
Execution guidance surface Possible but unoptimized

Our defensible advantage

Real, tested recipe content Deep tested library
Social proof and trust signals Ratings, reviews, UGC, editorial
Persistent personalization Can build across sessions
Dedicated cooking UI Optimized for the journey
Execution guidance surface Buildable once native recipes exist

12–18 Month Window

As LLMs gain access to real recipe databases, the trust gap narrows. The defensible moat is conviction + personalization + execution. Any one alone is replicable. All three combined — a product that knows you, surfaces things you can trust, and helps you cook them — is structurally harder to replicate.

The Phase 1 conviction and personalization work is both the right product investment and the right competitive investment. Build it faster.

The firing pattern we must prevent: When users consistently cannot find what to make, they route the decision to Google, TikTok, memory, or LLMs. When that habit forms, they don't use MyRecipes as a reference tool — they stop opening it entirely. They may land on a brand recipe page via Google search, but MyRecipes is bypassed completely in that flow. The decision relationship, once lost to another habit, is the hardest to reclaim.
Operating Principles

The guardrails that
protect the investment.

These are the rules that every product and roadmap decision should be tested against.

1

Build around answers, not options.

The product should collapse decisions, not multiply them. The standard is not "more relevant results." It is "the user committed to something."

2

Reliability is a strategy.

For reliability-gated 10x features, shipping before the accuracy threshold is met is a path to trust damage, not a faster path to value. Trust recovery is slow — measured in weeks and months, not sessions.

3

Keep AI invisible until visibility improves the outcome.

If removing the AI label does not change the user experience, remove it. No AI theater. No visible effort with no emotional impact.

4

The Established Home Cook is the constraint on default visibility.

50% of users. Most skeptical of explicit AI. If a feature would feel intrusive or performative to this segment, it is invisible by default and opt-in only. When in doubt, default to invisible.

5

The intelligence layer comes before the surface.

Build the semantic foundation before the features that depend on it. The interface is not the product. What the system understands is the product.

6

Treat recovery as a first-class feature, not an exception state.

In-the-moment rescue and plan recovery are where trust is won or permanently lost. They need the same investment attention as the core decision engine.

7

One intelligence system with multiple UX roles.

Context-specific surfaces are acceptable — and necessary. A fragmented intelligence stack is not. Every surface reads from and writes back to the shared brain.

8

Judge new ideas by whether they strengthen the core loops.

Two loops, one brain: Daily (Discover → Commit → Cook → Signal → Better discovery) and Weekly (Plan → Execute → Adapt → Smarter plan). Ideas outside both loops are not compounding the product.

9

Ship incremental features that matter. Don't wait for the complete picture.

The phase gates protect reliability-gated features — they do not mean nothing ships until an entire phase is done. Consumer-facing improvements within each phase should ship continuously. Visible progress is part of the product strategy, not separate from it.

The standard is not "AI-powered features."

The standard is: did we get them there?
