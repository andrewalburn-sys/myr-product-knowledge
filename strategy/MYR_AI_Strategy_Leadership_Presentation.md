## Section 1: The Audience

We grounded this in both **first-party audience data (ComScore)** and **behavioral journey research**, then synthesized that into actionable user segments.

As we all know our audience is not one "home cook" — it's fundamentally different user types with different needs across the journey.  These order with AI comfort level from low to high:


| Segment               | Share | One-line                              |
| --------------------- | ----- | ------------------------------------- |
| Established Home Cook | ~50%  | Experienced, but stuck in routines    |
| Weeknight Reducer     | ~25%  | Just trying to get dinner done        |
| Enthusiast            | ~10%  | Cooking for exploration and enjoyment |
| System Thinker        | ~15%  | Optimizing planning and workflows     |


These aren't just user segments — they're modes users move between depending on context. The same person can be a System Thinker on Sunday and a Weeknight Reducer by Wednesday.

## Section 2: Where the Real Problem Lives

The pain starts at Discovery and runs all the way through to execution. Finding the right recipe is part of the problem — but deciding, planning, shopping, and executing are where things fall apart most often and where the least has been built.

The biggest opportunity is helping users move from **"I need to make dinner"** → **"Dinner is done"** with as little friction as possible — across the entire journey.

## Section 3: The Journey

Once we understood the audience, we mapped the full cooking journey.

Users move from **discovering recipes** → **deciding what to make** → **planning and shopping** → **executing the meal**.

At each stage, there are real — and very different — pain points.

### Discover → Decide

Discovery and deciding are not cleanly separate steps — searching and browsing are active in both. The same user might browse passively for inspiration, then shift into active search once a constraint forms ("I have chicken, I need something fast"). The pain in this combined phase takes two forms:

- For the **Established Home Cook** and **Enthusiast**, the problem is qualitative: finding something genuinely worth making — meaningful novelty, not just more options, and enough context to feel confident committing to it.
- For the **Weeknight Reducer** and **System Thinker**, it's structural: too many results, not enough signal about which one actually fits their constraints. Deciding is the work, not browsing.

In both cases, the current experience doesn't distinguish between these modes — it returns the same results regardless of who is asking or why.

### Plan / Shop

Planning and shopping are fragmented — users are stitching together multiple tools, managing constraints manually, and rebuilding from scratch when anything changes. The shopping step is where planning quality gets exposed: lists are incomplete, ingredient overlap isn't managed, and the gap between "the plan" and "what's actually available" creates a second job at the store.

- For the **System Thinker**, this is the highest-friction handoff in the entire journey — a week's worth of planning can fall apart at the store if the list is wrong or ingredients don't overlap the way they assumed.
- For the **Weeknight Reducer**, it's a confidence problem: they need to know they can pull it off with what they have or one quick trip — uncertainty at this stage collapses the whole plan.
- For the **Established Home Cook**, familiar, right-sized quantities matter — recipes written for four don't automatically scale
- For the **Enthusiast**, specialty ingredients are a commitment risk — discovering something is unavailable after planning around it is a fast path to abandoning the dish entirely.

### Execute

Execution is rigid — recipes and plans don't adapt to real-world conditions. A missing ingredient, a schedule change, or one family member's rejection can collapse the whole evening.

- For the **Weeknight Reducer**, this is the most fragile moment in the journey. Recovery has to be instant and require no decisions 
- For the **System Thinker**, cooking itself is manageable when the plan holds. The real pain is disruption: when a day slips, existing tools can't rebalance the rest of the week without starting over.
- For the **Enthusiast**, the need is selective: technique depth on demand, and smart alternatives that preserve the spirit of the dish when something goes wrong — not a simplified way out.
- For the **Established Home Cook**, execution is largely self-sufficient. Friction shows up at the edges — adapting for health, scaling portions — and when things go wrong, she wants a trusted fallback, not an experiment.

---

## Section 4: Why AI

This is exactly the kind of problem AI is uniquely suited to solve.

This isn't a single task — it's a series of decisions, constraints, and adaptations across time. AI allows us to connect those steps, personalize them, and adapt in real time.

So instead of thinking about isolated features, we should be thinking about **how to support the entire journey — from idea to execution — as one connected system.**

That's the strategic frame for everything that follows.
